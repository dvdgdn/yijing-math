Examine the following statement and reason through its implications. It may challenge assumptions that shape your usual assessment of plausibility.

**Statement:** [INSERT STATEMENT]

**Question to resolve:** [INSERT QUESTION, OR “What follows if this statement is true?”]

**Supporting evidence, if available:** [INSERT EVIDENCE]

Give concise, checkable arguments. Keep the supplied statement, background assumptions, observations, and conclusions distinct.

1. **Clarify the claim.** State precisely what it asserts. Identify ambiguities without replacing it with a more convenient claim.

2. **Begin with a conditional analysis.** Accept the statement as a true premise for this stage. Determine what necessarily follows, what follows only with additional assumptions, and what remains undetermined. Agreement with the dominant paradigm is not a requirement for a valid implication.

3. **Locate any conflict.** Identify the specific commitments of the prevailing framework that conflict with the statement. If a contradiction arises, identify the premises that jointly produce it. Distinguish established observations from assumptions used to interpret those observations. Preserve unrelated knowledge wherever it remains consistent with the statement.

4. **Assess truth separately.** Now examine whether available evidence warrants accepting the statement. Present the strongest evidence and arguments on each side. Assess evidence by its methods, provenance, independence, reproducibility, and explanatory or predictive success. Explain what evidential work any appeal to expert consensus is doing. Apply consistent standards without assigning equal weight to unequal evidence.

5. **Check dependence on the framework.** For each major objection, ask whether it rests on a disputed paradigm assumption and whether it survives when that assumption is relaxed. Examine the alternative account just as critically: identify its unsupported assumptions, failures, and adjustments made merely to accommodate the claim.

6. **Separate credence from confidence.** Report your assessment of the statement’s truth separately from how well supported and robust that assessment is. Identify which reasonable changes in assumptions or evidence would change your verdict. Use numerical probabilities only if you can justify their precision.

7. **Identify a discriminating test.** Describe an observation, experiment, or proof that could distinguish the competing accounts. Explain how each possible result should affect the assessment. If no available test distinguishes them, state that limitation.

End with three clearly separated findings:

- What follows if the supplied statement is true.
- What the available evidence establishes about its truth.
- Whether its apparent improbability is supported by independent evidence, depends on disputed framework assumptions, or reflects both.

Acceptance and rejection must each be justified. If the evidence is insufficient, leave the truth assessment unresolved while completing the conditional analysis.
